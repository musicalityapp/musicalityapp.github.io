Chapter 01

Recognize Sound of Intervals (6 months)

Types of intervals

The 13 musical intervals are perfect unison, minor second, major second, minor third, major third, perfect fourth, tritone, perfect fifth, minor sixth, major sixth, minor seventh, major seventh, and perfect octave. It is important to recognize both the interval being played and whether it is ascending or descending.

This is a preview of how the first three ascending intervals sound. Do not memorize the intervals yet.

Intervals repeat across the fretboard starting at the octave. For example, the minor ninth and major ninth have the same recognizable sound as the minor second and major second, respectively. Therefore, it is preferable to name intervals from unison to octave. On this webpage, you should not refer to them as ninths, tenths, or elevenths.

Knowing all intervals on the fretboard

In this stage, you'll focus on not just hearing intervals but also locating them. Now that you can identify intervals by ear, the next step is to learn their positions on the guitar fretboard. You'll first focus on the last four strings (G, D, A, E), then move on to the rest. These are the shapes used on the fretboard.

Knowing all intervals on the keyboard

In this stage, you'll focus not only on hearing intervals but also on locating them. Now that you can identify intervals by ear, the next step is to learn the number of steps needed to create a specific interval on the keyboard.

Intervals vary in shape across the keyboard, but the number of steps remains consistent. For this reason, we don’t rely on the visual shape of an interval on the keyboard (unlike on guitar and bass). Instead, we rely on the distance between the notes, measured in steps.

Chapter 03

Recognizing Interval Sound and Position (1 year)

Select your instrument

Choose Guitar, Piano, or Bass at the top of the page to see the instrument-specific content for this chapter.

Recalling all intervals everyday

Remember to play all the intervals by ear at least once every day(sing an interval in your head and then play it). For example, play a major second, then a major third, then a perfect fourth, and so on. By the time you finish the major intervals, move on to the minor intervals. This is where music theory comes in handy. You should try to start the minor scale from a convenient position—in this case, two and a half steps below the starting note, or at the sixth interval.

fretboard showing the shapes of the minor and major scale

Sing all major and perfect intervals starting from any note. When you finish playing them, go to the sixth degree (the sixth interval) by singing a major sixth above the root note(the first note you played) or by singing a minor third below the root note . Once there, play the minor intervals from the minor third (m3) to the minor seventh (m7). Finally, move up a major second and sing a minor second (m2).

The loop

We need to understand a loop. First you need to recognize the interval by ear, find it in the fretboard and then play the interval. For every loop, you start again where you left off.

Sing and play , simultaneously:

  • ✅ One interval (2 weeks)
  • ✅ Two intervals (2 weeks)
  • ✅ Three intervals (2 weeks)
  • ✅ More than three intervals (2 weeks)

Can hear and play (use this dictation app):

  • ✅ Two intervals (2 weeks)
  • ✅ Three intervals (2 weeks)
  • ✅ More than three intervals (2 weeks)

Can sing over chord progressions (use the app chordchord):

  • ✅ One interval (2 weeks)
  • ✅ Two intervals (2 weeks)
  • ✅ Three intervals (2 weeks)
  • ✅ More than three intervals (2 weeks)

Note: Practice at 70 BPM. Faster BPM is harder.

Here first you sing anything you think sounds cool to you (the complete thing — do not use the loop), after finishing singing all, use the loop to play it.

Thinking then playing (gain precision):

  • ✅ Make words (2 weeks)
  • ✅ Think at least three notes then play them (2 weeks)
  • ✅ Make phrases (2 weeks)
  • ✅ Join notes into meaningful phrases (2 weeks)

Thinking and playing immediately (exchange precision for speed):

  • ✅ Play in real time (≤1s per note)

playing using octaves

Practice playing in octaves (disco style) for 1 minute a day using only minor sevenths, major sixths, and major sevenths. This will help you accurately feel intervals that are very far apart.
